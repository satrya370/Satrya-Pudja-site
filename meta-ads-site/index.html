Two Meta Ads campaigns for the same service, because two buyers are not the same buyer.

Between July and September 2026 I sold landing page building as a freelance service to two kinds of local businesses in Indonesia: individual tour guides in Bali and small service businesses like AC installation. I ran them as separate campaigns with different geographic scope and different budgets instead of one generic ad for "landing pages" pointed at everyone.

What I was actually selling

The service itself was the same both times: I build a landing page for a small local business that doesn't have one, mostly people running a service business off word of mouth or a WhatsApp number. But the two groups I sold it to don't behave the same way, so I never ran them under one ad.

The first group was individual tour guides in Bali who take direct bookings instead of going through an agency. The second was local service businesses you'd find in any Indonesian city, AC installation being the clearest example. Same pitch underneath ("you need a page people can find and book you from"), but a tour guide in Ubud and an AC technician in Surabaya are not looking for the same thing, and they don't cost the same to reach.

Tour guides: Bali only, bigger budget

I kept this campaign's geographic scope tight, Bali and nowhere else, because that's where the actual buyers are. A tighter map meant the budget didn't have to stretch across a whole country, so I could afford to spend more per person in that smaller pool and still reach a meaningful share of it.

Local service businesses: Indonesia wide, smaller budget

AC installation and businesses like it exist in every city, so I opened the targeting up nationally instead of picking one region to bet on. That wider net meant a lower budget had to cover a lot more ground, which was the point: this campaign was about finding where the demand actually showed up before committing more spend to any one place.

How I ran each campaign

  • Objectives came before creative. Before writing a single ad, I decided what each campaign was actually trying to get someone to do, then set the budget and audience around that, instead of picking a budget number first and figuring out the rest afterward.
  • Creative was written for the buyer, not the service. The tour guide ads talked about getting direct bookings and looking legitimate to foreign tourists checking a profile before messaging. The service business ads talked about being findable when someone searches for the service instead of asking a neighbor for a recommendation. Same underlying product, different reason to care.
  • I watched Meta Ads Manager, not vanity numbers. Reach and impressions told me if an ad was even getting seen, but the number I actually cared about was whether people engaged with it enough to message or click through, since that's the only thing that turns into an actual lead.
  • Underperforming creative got replaced, not tweaked forever. When a message angle or image wasn't pulling engagement after a fair run, I swapped it for a different angle rather than making small edits to something that wasn't working in the first place.

Why I split it this way

Running both audiences under one campaign would have meant averaging two different buying decisions into one set of numbers, which hides exactly the thing you need to see: which audience is actually responding and to what. Splitting scope and budget by segment meant I could tell early which one needed a different message, a different image, or more money behind it, instead of finding out three weeks in that one half of a blended campaign had been quietly wasting spend the whole time.
